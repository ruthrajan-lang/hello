Xfinity Shield: earning the upgrade by fixing the free tier first

Shield bundles network security, parental controls and WiFi motion sensing into the Xfinity app for free, with a $15 a month tier that adds a camera and sensor. I looked at what stops people trusting the free tier, redesigned those flows, and then designed an upgrade path that asks at the right moment.

Context

A retention product inside a billing app

Comcast launched Xfinity Shield on August 18, 2026. The free tier, WiFi Shield, comes with internet plans that use an Advanced Gateway. Shield Select adds an indoor camera with person, pet, vehicle and package detection, a door sensor, cloud video and emergency help for $15 a month.

Coverage of the launch frames it as a response to broadband customers leaving. That makes the design problem two-sided: the free tier has to be good enough to keep people, and credible enough that paying for more feels safe.

Research

Three inputs, one pattern

I read Play Store reviews from August to November 2026, walked through the live app on November 18 and captured 28 screens, and compared Shield with five rival apps. The pattern across all three: people are not asking for more features. They are locked out, lost, or unsure what the product promises.

Locked out

An "update required" screen returns after updating, with no newer build in the store. Sign-in loops and error pages block bill pay.

Parental controls moved

Walkthrough
Strengths
  • The Gateway is already in the home, so the free tier needs no new hardware.
  • Motion sensing without cameras.
Weaknesses
  • App stability and navigation dominate reviews.
  • Marketing language runs ahead of what the product does.
Opportunities
  • Most ISPs have left home security.
  • Ring's 2026 privacy backlash leaves room for a privacy-first position.
Threats
  • Trust: the launch followed a $117.5 million breach settlement.
  • Every rival offers one app for everything.

Reviews skew negative and are Android-only, so I treated them as themes, not proportions. The full SWOT covers Ring, SimpliSafe, ADT+, Arlo and Wyze with sources.

Design principles

Six rules I held every screen to

  1. Never lock people out of essentials. Paying a bill, checking an outage and pausing WiFi work even when an update is pending.
  2. Say what it is and what it isn't, first. The "not monitored" message belongs on the opening screen, not behind a checkbox.
  3. Two taps for what parents do daily. Pause, resume and bonus time sit on the Home tab.
  4. Every alert has an off switch and a next step. Act from the notification, and undo it if you tapped wrong.
  5. A person is always reachable. The assistant is one option, never the gate.
  6. Ask for the upgrade when it's worth something. And make "no" as easy as "yes".

How I built it

Research to live prototype in two days

I worked as a designer-builder, with Claude Code as the build partner.

  • Research. I captured the live app, gathered reviews and launch coverage, and used Claude to sort them into themes. I checked each theme back against the source text and dropped claims the reviews didn't support.
  • Recreate. The current screens were rebuilt from my screenshots, so each change can be compared with what ships today.
  • Design in code. The prototype is one HTML file with no framework. I directed it screen by screen, sending annotated screenshots for padding, alignment, icons and type.
  • Ship. It deploys to Cloudflare from GitHub on every push, so each round of feedback was live in about a minute.

What I'd do next: test the pause and alert flows with five parents, confirm with engineering that setup can finish in the background, and take the privacy screen to legal, since every statement on it is a proposal.
